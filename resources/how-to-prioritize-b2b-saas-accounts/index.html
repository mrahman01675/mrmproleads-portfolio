ARTICLE #06 · RESEARCH QUALITY ASSURANCE

How to Measure the Quality of Your B2B Account Research

A practical framework for checking whether your account research is accurate, evidence-based, up to date and genuinely useful to your sales team.

By Mahbubur R. · MRMProLeads

A spreadsheet can look impressive. It may contain company names, websites, decision-makers, job titles, email addresses and plenty of notes. But before your sales team relies on it, there is a more important question to ask: can you trust what is in it?

Are the companies still active? Do the people listed still work there? Can the important details be traced back to credible sources? And does the research give a salesperson a clear reason to engage with the account?

A large database is not automatically a high-quality one. It can contain outdated information, assumptions presented as facts, or contacts who have little connection to the buying decision. The value of research comes from the reliability and usefulness of the information—not the number of fields completed.

That is where quality assurance (QA) comes in. QA is not the process of deciding which accounts to target. It is the process of checking whether the information is dependable enough to support account selection and sales activity.

This guide explains six practical factors for assessing B2B account research, introduces a simple weighted scorecard, and shows how to catch issues before they reach your sales workflow.

What Is High-Quality B2B Account Research?

High-quality B2B account research gives a sales team a reliable picture of a company, the people relevant to a potential purchase, and the business context needed to make an informed next move.

In practice, it should answer six straightforward questions:

  • Is the information accurate?
  • Can important claims be traced to credible sources?
  • Is the information current enough for its intended use?
  • Is it relevant to the client’s ideal customer profile (ICP), offer and sales objective?
  • Does it cover the agreed research requirements?
  • Can the sales team act on it?

Twenty-five populated fields do not necessarily make a better record than twelve. If the extra details are guessed, outdated or commercially irrelevant, they add noise rather than value. The aim is to provide the right information, show what supports it, and make any gaps clear.

Six Factors That Make Account Research Reliable

A useful quality review looks beyond how complete a spreadsheet appears. These six factors help a researcher and reviewer judge whether a record is dependable and fit for purpose.

1. Accuracy: Are the facts correct?

Start with the company. Is it the right business? Does the official website match the company being researched? Is the business still active, and does it offer the products or services recorded in the research?

Then check the people. Confirm that the contact’s name and current role are supported by available evidence. Someone listed in an old directory may have moved on, and a senior title alone does not prove that the person owns the problem your client is trying to solve.

Keep email verification separate from buyer verification. An address that appears deliverable does not prove that it belongs to the intended person or that the person is a decision-maker. Never present a guessed email pattern as a verified public email address. If an address cannot be confirmed, say so clearly.

2. Traceability: Can the claims be checked?

Research is much easier to review when another person can follow the evidence without repeating the entire investigation. For each important claim, record the source URL, the specific detail it supports and, where useful, the date it was checked.

Not every source proves every claim. A company homepage may confirm its product offering, for example, but it may not establish that the business is hiring, expanding into a new market or using a particular technology. The source must support the exact statement attached to it.

Use primary sources wherever practical, such as:

  • Official company websites and product pages
  • Company newsrooms and announcements
  • Careers pages and current job listings
  • Official leadership or team pages
  • The individual’s professional profile

Secondary sources can add useful context. However, if a claim could influence an outreach decision, look for the strongest available evidence. A source link is useful only when it actually supports the claim.

3. Freshness: Is the information up to date?

Some information remains useful for months, while other details can change quickly. A product description may stay accurate for a long time; job titles, leadership changes, recruitment activity, expansion news and buying signals may not.

Consider how quickly each type of information is likely to change. Record when time-sensitive details were checked and revisit them before using older research in a new campaign.

Do not assume that information is current simply because the webpage is still accessible. Check its publication date or current status wherever possible.

4. Relevance: Does the information matter to the sales objective?

A detail can be completely accurate and still have little commercial value. If you discover that a software company uses a particular technology, for example, that fact matters only if it connects to the client’s offer, an integration requirement or a meaningful use case.

Research should reflect the client’s ICP, product or service, target geography, sales motion, intended buyer and account-selection criteria.

It is also important to separate observations from hypotheses. If a company announces that it is entering a new region, that announcement is an observation. Suggesting that the company may need help identifying target accounts in that region is a hypothesis. It may be worth investigating, but it must not be presented as a confirmed buying need.

5. Completeness: Does it cover the agreed scope?

Completeness does not mean filling every possible column. It means providing the information required for the project. Depending on the scope, that may include:

  • Company identity and ICP fit
  • Relevant business context
  • Key stakeholders and likely decision-makers
  • Public contact details or contact channels
  • Evidence for important claims
  • Required exclusions and duplicate checks

Buying-committee research should focus on roles connected to the purchase. A short list of well-supported stakeholders is usually more useful than a long list of unrelated job titles.

Before research begins, agree on what a completed record should contain. That gives both the researcher and reviewer a shared standard and makes missing information easier to identify.

6. Actionability: Does it make the next step clear?

This is where research quality becomes especially useful to a sales team. A good record explains why the account was included, what is known, what remains unknown, and who may be connected to the relevant business problem.

It should also help the salesperson understand what to verify or do next. For example, a record might identify a relevant business development lead, link to a recent company announcement and explain why that context could matter to the client’s offer. If the possible need is still a hypothesis, the record should say so.

If a salesperson has to repeat the entire research process just to understand why an account was selected, the record may be accurate but it is not actionable enough.

A Practical B2B Research Quality Assurance Scorecard

A scorecard gives researchers and reviewers a consistent way to assess quality. Instead of relying on a general impression, they can evaluate the same six factors against agreed criteria.

The weights below are a suggested operating model, not an industry standard. Adjust them to suit the project, the deliverable and the potential consequences of getting something wrong.

FactorWeightWhat the reviewer should assess
Accuracy25Company, person, role and contact details are correct and supported.
Traceability20Important claims can be checked against sources that support them.
Freshness15Time-sensitive information has been reviewed recently enough.
Relevance15The research supports the ICP, offer and intended sales decision.
Completeness15Agreed research questions and relevant stakeholder roles are covered.
Actionability10Context, uncertainty and next steps are clear.
Total100Suggested weighted score

Score each factor from 0 to 5, then convert the result to its assigned weight. A score of 0 means the required evidence is missing or unusable. A score of 5 means the criterion has been fully met and can be demonstrated.

Do not give a record a high accuracy score simply because every field contains information. The facts need to be supported. Likewise, adding a source URL does not earn full marks for traceability if the page does not prove the claim.

The purpose of the scorecard is to assess how reliable and useful the research is—not how polished the spreadsheet looks.

Set Acceptance Criteria Before Research Starts

Many quality problems can be prevented before the first company is researched. If the researcher and reviewer have different ideas about what makes a complete record, avoidable gaps are likely to appear in the final deliverable.

Agree on a short set of acceptance criteria in advance:

  • Mandatory and optional fields: Decide which fields are essential and which are helpful extras. A missing optional detail should not automatically make a record unusable; a missing mandatory fact may.
  • Evidence requirements: Identify which claims need sources, such as company identity, current role, relevant business signals and facts that could affect outreach.
  • Freshness expectations: Set expectations by data type. A person’s current role may need a more recent check than a general product description.
  • Verified, Inferred and Unknown: Agree how to label each type of information. This helps reviewers see what has been established and what remains an assumption or gap.
  • Exclusions: Define how to flag inactive companies, duplicates, previously contacted accounts and businesses outside the agreed commercial model.

Once everyone agrees on what is required, quality becomes easier to measure and feedback can be much more specific.

How to Conduct a Lightweight Research Quality Check

You do not need a complicated compliance programme to improve research quality. A consistent review process can catch the issues that matter most and help improve the workflow over time.

  1. Review the brief. Confirm the ICP, target geography, company criteria, stakeholder roles, source requirements and intended use of the research.
  2. Review completed records. Check all high-impact fields and a reasonable sample of the remaining information. For a small paid pilot, reviewing every record may be practical.
  3. Open the sources. Do not rely only on notes or copied URLs. Confirm that each page supports the exact statement recorded.
  4. Categorise errors. Getting the company or buyer wrong is serious. A small formatting inconsistency usually is not.
  5. Give specific feedback. Identify the field, explain what is wrong, state what evidence is needed and describe the correction.
  6. Look for patterns. Repeated issues may point to an unclear brief, weak source selection, insufficient training or a process problem.
  7. Recheck corrected records. Treat an issue as resolved only after the revised information and supporting evidence have been reviewed.

This approach does more than identify errors. It helps researchers understand where the process needs to improve.

Critical, Major and Minor Research Errors

Not all errors carry the same risk. Spending the same amount of time on every issue can distract reviewers from problems that could misdirect outreach. Classify errors according to their likely impact.

Critical errors

These can cause an account to be selected incorrectly or contacted on a false basis. Examples include:

  • Incorrect company identity
  • Wrong person or current role
  • Guessed contact details presented as verified
  • No evidence for a material buying signal
  • Major mismatch with the agreed ICP
  • A known duplicate or excluded account

Critical errors should be corrected before the record is used for outreach.

Major errors

These reduce the reliability or usefulness of a record. Examples include outdated company context, missing sources for important claims, incomplete coverage of required stakeholders, or hypotheses presented as established facts. Resolve these issues or clearly document the limitation before accepting the record.

Minor errors

These generally affect presentation rather than the underlying research, such as inconsistent formatting, unclear wording or optional fields left blank when they are not essential. They can usually be handled during normal editorial review.

The key is to match the review effort to the potential impact of the error.

Example: Why a Complete Spreadsheet Can Still Fail Quality Review

Imagine two research records prepared for the same B2B campaign.

Record A: Looks complete, but the evidence is weak

It contains a company name, website, founder’s name, job title and email address. At first glance, it looks ready to use. But the job title came from an old directory, the email was guessed from a common company pattern, and the expansion signal has no supporting source.

Result: It should fail quality review. The most important details cannot be trusted.

Record B: Fewer fields, stronger evidence

The company and its product offering are confirmed on the official website. The relevant person’s current role is supported by a professional profile. The public contact channel is clearly identified, and the business-context note links to a company announcement. Anything that remains unknown is marked.

Result: This is the stronger deliverable. A reviewer can check the claims, understand the limitations and decide what should happen next.

Record B shows what useful research looks like: not necessarily more information, but better-supported information.

Common Mistakes That Reduce Research Quality

Even experienced researchers can fall into habits that make a deliverable look more complete without making it more reliable. Watch for these common mistakes:

  • Measuring quality by volume: More rows and fields do not automatically mean better research.
  • Adding sources without checking them: A source is useful only when it supports the statement.
  • Confusing deliverability with identity: An email passing a verification check does not prove that the intended person is the right buyer.
  • Applying one freshness rule to everything: Different types of information change at different speeds.
  • Hiding uncertainty: An incomplete but honest record is safer than one filled with unsupported assumptions.
  • Focusing on formatting: A polished spreadsheet cannot compensate for incorrect company or contact information.
  • Ignoring feedback: Repeated errors should lead to improvements in the brief, source selection or research process.

A good QA process makes these issues visible and gives the team a way to address them.

How Quality Assurance Supports Better Sales Decisions

Quality assurance and account qualification are connected, but they are not the same thing. QA checks whether the information is reliable. Qualification and prioritisation use that information to decide whether an account is commercially worth pursuing.

A company record can be fully verified and still be a poor commercial fit. Equally, an account may look promising, but if its key facts or buyer details cannot be substantiated, it should not progress without further review.

Keeping these activities separate creates a more disciplined sales process and helps teams avoid treating an interesting observation as proof of buying intent.

Reliable research cannot guarantee a positive reply or a sale. It can, however, give your team a more transparent and dependable basis for deciding where to focus its sales effort.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is considered high-quality B2B account research?

High-quality B2B account research is accurate, verifiable, up to date, relevant, sufficiently complete and actionable. Important details should be supported by credible sources, and any unknowns should be clearly identified.

How can you ensure B2B account research is accurate?

Check the company, its business activity, products or services, contacts, current roles and relevant contact details against reliable sources. Treat email deliverability and buyer identity as separate checks; one does not prove the other.

Why is source traceability important?

Traceability lets a researcher or salesperson see where a claim came from and whether the source actually supports it. It also makes errors easier to investigate and correct.

How frequently should B2B account research be updated?

It depends on the data. Roles, recruitment activity, expansion news and other time-sensitive indicators may need regular checks. More stable company information may remain useful for longer. Recheck key details before using older research in a campaign.

What is the difference between research quality and ICP fit?

Research quality measures how reliable and useful the information is. ICP fit measures how closely the company matches the client’s ideal customer profile. An account can be thoroughly researched and still be a poor commercial fit.

How should research errors be categorised?

Classify errors as critical, major or minor. Critical errors can misdirect account selection or outreach. Major errors weaken reliability or completeness. Minor errors usually affect presentation or clarity.

What should a B2B research QA scorecard include?

A practical scorecard can assess six factors: accuracy, traceability, freshness, relevance, completeness and actionability. The suggested weights in this guide are a starting point and should be adjusted to suit the team’s requirements and the risks of the project.

Conclusion

High-quality B2B account research is not about how much information sits in a spreadsheet. It is about whether that information is reliable and useful.

Accuracy helps prevent factual mistakes. Traceability lets people check the claims. Freshness keeps important details current. Relevance connects the research to the sales objective. Completeness shows whether the agreed requirements have been covered. Actionability helps the sales team decide what to do next.

Define what good research looks like before the work begins. Focus on evidence rather than appearances, take serious errors seriously, and use review findings to improve the process.

No research framework can guarantee that every account will become a customer. A structured QA process does give your team a more dependable basis for deciding which accounts deserve attention and how to approach them.
